HackHarvard is a 36-hour undergraduate hackathon hosted at Harvard University, bringing more than 500 students from 30+ universities to campus each October. As Director of Design, I ran the team that owned how the event looked and spoke: the promotional campaign, the brand, the website and everything printed for the weekend.

For years, marketing had been handled by whoever had time, usually engineers. The result was thin imagery, little visual consistency and a logo nobody could read: in conversations with past members, most of them couldn't say what the old mark was supposed to mean.

The board saw the cost. Applications were coming in because of the Harvard name, not because of the hackathon itself, and next to the big contemporary hackathons HackHarvard looked unremarkable. They asked me for a complete revamp of the brand assets and identity, so the event could stand on its own.

Design sat in the middle of the organization. Sponsorship needed logos placed on the site and the shirts, logistics needed signage and schedules, and outreach needed a steady stream of posts. Part of my job was lining our deliverables up with all three teams so nothing shipped twice or off-brand.

{% endcall %} {# ===== Branding ===== #} {% call cs.section("Branding and Logo", lead="Rebuilding the identity from zero to one") %}

Before any campaign work, the brand itself had to be rebuilt from scratch. The new mark puts the emphasis on the letters H and A, stacked into a single monogram with a small chip-like spark, and a nod to pi and math. I drew it on a grid so it holds its shape from a favicon up to a banner.

Around the monogram I built a set of lockups for different jobs. The flat off-season version handles the favicon, official documents and small imprints like post logos. The 2026 version carries the year's theme: utopia, stardust, translucency, the moon and the galaxy.

Hack to the Moon takes the campus's domes, cupolas, columns and stairs and rebuilds them as glowing gradient geometry among planets, moons and stars. The monogram itself became architecture: in the key art, the H is a tower and the C is a crescent moon.

{% endcall %} {% call cs.row("fit", caption="Key art and the Hack to the Moon lockups, used on season only for the Instagram profile picture and the website header.") %} {{ cs.card(img('design', 'hackharvard/keyart.jpg'), "HackHarvard key art: the monogram built from a tower and crescent moon", ar=1) }} {{ cs.card(img('design', 'hackharvard/lockups.png'), "Four Hack to the Moon lockups pairing the tower-and-crescent monogram with serif and bold wordmarks, plus usage instructions", ar=0.993) }} {% endcall %} {# ===== System ===== #} {% call cs.section("The System", lead="Few enough rules that five people could follow them") %}

I kept the identity deliberately small so the whole team could produce on-brand work without checking with me on every piece:
— Palette: near-black navy, a hot magenta accent, teal to ocean blue, cream glows, and yellow only for the wordmark and small highlights.
— Signature gradient: navy → ocean blue → teal → mauve → pink, used on the monogram, the architecture and the merch.
— Two typefaces, everywhere: Makcasa for display headlines and the lockup, AV Estiana for body copy, labels, dates and every number.

{% endcall %} {# ===== Campaign ===== #} {% call cs.section("Campaign", lead="A redesigned content strategy built on the same world") %}

Every post drew from the same set of towers, moons and rockets, so the feed read as one story from announcement to applications. Each Instagram post drew 3× more interactions than prior posts, and followers grew 30.3%, from 3,663 to 4,772.

The team built the site as a reusable component package: every color is a design token, the decorations are a shared SVG library, and the same pieces feed social graphics and event materials. Each section became a place in the world: About sits inside a temple, Tracks floats on an island with a satellite you can spin, and each FAQ you open lights a lamp on the arch gate.

The gradient monogram works on screen but not on every material, so each item got its own version: full color for the stickers and the sublimated lanyards, two totes (one carrying the Hack to the Moon artwork, one the monogram), and five flat Pantone spots for the enamel pin.
