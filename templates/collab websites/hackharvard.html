HackHarvard is a 36-hour undergraduate hackathon hosted at Harvard University, bringing more than 500 students from 30+ universities to campus each October. As Director of Design, I ran the team that owned how the event looked and spoke: the promotional campaign, the brand, the website and everything printed for the weekend.

For years, marketing had been handled by usually engineers since we prioritized tech quality, which led to little visual consistency and a logo nobody could read. In conversations with past members to improve current imagery, most of them couldn't say what the old mark was supposed to mean.

The board saw the cost as we stood next to the big contemporary hackathons and looked unremarkable. They asked me for a complete revamp of the brand assets and identity, so the event could stand on its own.

{% endcall %} {# ===== Branding ===== #} {% call cs.section("Branding and Logo", lead="Rebuilding the identity from zero to one") %}

Before any campaign work, the brand itself had to be rebuilt from scratch. The new mark puts the emphasis on the letters H and A, stacked into a single monogram with a small chip-like spark, and a nod to pi and math. I drew it on a grid so it holds its shape from a favicon up to a banner.

Around the monogram I built a set of lockups for different jobs. The flat off-season version handles the favicon, official documents and small imprints like post logos. The 2026 version carries the year's theme: utopia, stardust, translucency, the moon and the galaxy.

Hack to the Moon takes the campus's domes, cupolas, columns and stairs and rebuilds them as glowing gradient geometry among planets, moons and stars. The monogram itself became architecture. In the key art, the H is a tower and the C is a crescent moon.

I kept the identity deliberately small so the whole team could produce on-brand work without checking with me on every piece:
— Palette: near-black navy, a hot magenta accent, teal to ocean blue, cream glows, and yellow only for the wordmark and small highlights.
— Signature gradient: navy → ocean blue → teal → mauve → pink, used on the monogram, the architecture and the merch.
— Two typefaces, everywhere: Makcasa for display headlines and the lockup, AV Estiana for body copy, labels, dates and every number.

{% endcall %} {# ===== Campaign ===== #} {% call cs.section("Campaign", lead="Redesigned content strategy") %}

Every post drew from the same set of towers, moons and rockets, so the feed read as one story from announcement to applications. Last year's whole campaign was a single launch post, and this was our first year without MLH promoting the event, so our own feed was the main way hackers found us.

Launch against launch, "HackHarvard is back" drew 3.4× the interactions of last year's post (670 → 2,254), with 4.2× the likes and 6.5× the saves. It wasn't a one-off: all three campaign posts beat last year's on likes, comments, reposts, shares and saves, holding at 500+ likes and 400+ saves each. Followers grew 30.3%, from 3,663 to 4,772.

{# ===== Leading the team ===== #} {% call cs.section("Legacy", lead="Built to outlast the year") %}

From April to October I set direction, split the work across seven designers and engineers, and kept our output in step with sponsorship, logistics and outreach. Before handing off, I wrote the site's README as a guide for next year's team. It separates what every HackHarvard site needs, no matter the theme, from this year's theme-specific rules, so the next team can swap the theme without rebuilding the foundation.

I planned an internal component tracker with Codex and built it, and its artifacts, with Claude Code, so both AI agents and teammates can read it. That let members of other teams ship on-brand graphics quickly, for example mid-conversation with a sponsor.

With the tracker and the reusable package, a post went from 3–4 days to 1–2 days on average. Questions to the design team halved, from 4.2 to 2.1 on average. Before, they were mostly about tracking: where to upload, where the assets were, when something was due and who it was for. After, they were mostly about approving the quality of a design.
