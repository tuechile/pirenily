A prosthetic leg is a system. On average, nine separate components come together for one fitting. If one part is missing, delayed or mislabeled, the patient goes home without a leg.

2ft Prosthetics works across 10 countries, sending donated components from U.S. manufacturers and clinics to amputees who can't otherwise get them. Most of the work is done by volunteers (students, clinicians and prosthetists) across time zones, languages and patchy internet.

As 2ft grew, collecting parts stopped being the hard part.
Keeping track of them was.

{% endcall %} {{ cs.figure(img('ux', 't4sg/redes.webp'), "2ft inventory system redesign overview", zoom=False) }} {% call cs.section("Problem", lead="Fragile Management Systems") %}

Inventory lived in spreadsheets, Notion pages and manual cross-checks. On paper, everything was recorded. In practice, volunteers couldn't answer basic questions:

1. What do we actually have right now?
2. Where is this component stored?
3. Has it shipped? Has it been fitted?
4. Who donated this — and how do we report impact back?

{% endcall %} {% call cs.row("3", caption="The spreadsheets 2ft ran its inventory on before the redesign.") %} {{ cs.card(img('ux', 't4sg/spreadsheets.png'), "Spreadsheet: general inventory view") }} {{ cs.card(img('ux', 't4sg/sheet2.png'), "Spreadsheet: component tracking tab") }} {{ cs.card(img('ux', 't4sg/sheet3.png'), "Spreadsheet: summary of requested components") }} {% endcall %} {% call cs.section("Interview & User Research") %}

We interviewed 2ft's president, treasurer and three volunteers who update inventory in the field. Instead of hypotheticals, we asked them to show us real work:

1. Screen-shared walkthroughs of current spreadsheets
2. How updates happen under time pressure
3. Where mistakes, duplication, or confusion creep in
4. What happens when Wi-Fi disappears

We also compared existing inventory tools, including Airtable, Sortly, Notion and Odoo.

{% endcall %} {# §12 Bespoke: Interview Insights explorer #} {% call cs.block() %}

Interview Highlights

President Inventory

“The same part exists in three different places.”

Inventory lives across multiple spreadsheets, tabs, and naming conventions, making it hard to know whether a component is available, used, or misplaced.

Design implication: one canonical inventory source with consistent component IDs and clear status states (in storage, on trip, fitted, returned).

President Labeling

“You have to already know the system to use it.”

Status labels and categories are inconsistent, undocumented, and difficult for new volunteers to understand without direct guidance.

Design implication: standardized terminology, inline definitions, and guardrails that prevent mislabeling during data entry.

President Operations

“I just want to know what we have right now.”

There is no reliable real-time snapshot of inventory — checking availability means digging through spreadsheets and asking people directly.

Design implication: a default dashboard showing current inventory counts with last-updated timestamps and editor attribution.

Volunteer Offline

“I usually write it down and update it later.”

Clinics, travel days, and storage sites often lack reliable internet, forcing volunteers to track parts on paper or in personal notes.

Design implication: offline-first data entry with visible sync states and safe conflict resolution when reconnecting.

Volunteer Edit anxiety

“I’m worried I’ll mess something up.”

Volunteers hesitate to update records because it’s unclear who edited what last, or whether changes can be undone.

Design implication: edit history, change previews, and clear save confirmation to support confident collaboration.

Volunteer Navigation

“I never know where to look.”

Information is spread across tabs and columns with no clear hierarchy, making even simple lookups slow.

Design implication: task-oriented navigation (search, add, move, review) instead of spreadsheet-style layouts.

Treasurer Reporting

“We rebuild reports every year.”

Donor and inventory reports are assembled manually because fields aren’t consistent or comparable across years.

Design implication: normalized donor fields, timestamps, and export-ready reports for year-over-year comparison.

Treasurer Accountability

“I don’t directly manage inventory — but I’m responsible for it.”

Components represent real financial value, yet inventory tracking doesn’t clearly support auditing or accountability.

Design implication: traceability from donor → component → usage to support financial oversight without extra manual work.

The real bottleneck
Updating is harder than tracking
The default environment
Internet is unreliable (or absent)
The trust requirement
“Who changed this?” must be obvious
{% endcall %} {{ cs.callout("What we learned quickly", display=False, text="Most existing tools failed in the same four ways: they assumed stable internet, crammed too much onto each screen, mixed logistics with technical data, and took new volunteers too long to learn.") }} {% call cs.section("Idea", lead="Five principles from the affinity map") %}

— Clarity over completeness
— Fast entry over perfect data
— Offline-first by default
— Low cognitive load for new volunteers
— Traceability without visual overload

{% endcall %} {% call cs.media_text(img('ux', 't4sg/affmap.png'), "Impact vs effort matrix", heading="Ideation", lead="Feature decisions by Impact × Effort") %}

We ranked every feature idea by how fast it would cut uncertainty. On an impact × effort map, the quick wins went first, and more powerful features waited until the core flows worked.

That kept the product simple enough for a new volunteer, with room to grow as 2ft scales.

{% endcall %} {{ cs.img_caption("Clarity and offline reliability first; power features later.") }} {{ cs.section("Feature Rollout", index="Rollout") }} {{ cs.rollout([ ("Phase 1", "Core Inventory Reliability", "Simple viewing, terminology, edit component."), ("Phase 2", "Deeper Traceability", "Edit history, comments, richer categorization."), ("Phase 3", "Scalability Features", "Add component flow, filters, helpers."), ]) }} {% call cs.section("User Flow", lead="View / Edit / Add Component Flow") %}

I wrote the user journeys and owned the end-to-end flow for how a volunteer views a component, makes a safe edit, and adds a new component without breaking traceability.

The decisions I made, and why:
— Primary actions (view history, edit, add) are always in reach, never buried in a menu
— Edits open in inline popups with clear save states, because fear of breaking records kept people from updating them
— Technical fields use dropdowns and placeholders, not free text, so the same part never gets three names
— Every record shows who changed it and when, so "who changed this?" has an answer

{% endcall %} {% call cs.media_text(img('ux', 't4sg/userflow.png'), "User flow: View Component", heading="Flow Breakdown", lead="View Component", flow=True) %}

The component page answers "where is it and what state is it in?" first (logistics), and only then the technical details. Collapsible sections keep the rest out of the way.

The page reads in four zones, in the order a volunteer needs them: a sticky profile, then logistics → technical → manufacturer.

The blue and yellow design adds structure without noise: logistics → technical → manufacturer sections, collapsible panels, inline edit popups and a visible history.

{% endcall %} {{ cs.mockups([ (img('ux', 't4sg/hifi view.webp'), "Hi-fi view component (blue/yellow)"), (img('ux', 't4sg/hifi edit.webp'), "Hi-fi edit component inline popup (blue/yellow)"), (img('ux', 't4sg/hifi add.webp'), "Hi-fi add component (blue/yellow)"), ], caption="(1) View component, (2) Edit component, (3) Add component") }} {% call cs.section("Testing & Feedback", lead="Where the design got smarter, fast") %}

What worked: starting from the component type. Each type has its own properties, so picking the type first felt far more natural than showing every spreadsheet field and making volunteers guess.

What stakeholders asked for: less typing. Autofill, a stock image when a part has no photo, and adding new fields on the fly all came up.

Permissions: changing a field can cause mix-ups across the whole organization, so stakeholders wanted clear roles, for example who can edit fields and who can only edit values.

What we flagged for the build: open backend questions, such as whether field management needs its own admin page, and what autofill would pull from (model number, a manufacturer database or past entries).

After testing the prototype, the president told us:
“I don’t feel like I’m going to break anything.”

{% endcall %} {% call cs.section("Reflection", lead="What this project taught me") %}

This was my first UX project as a CS major :), so I was learning the frameworks while using them. The biggest lesson: designing for social impact is less about adding features and more about removing uncertainty, so a volunteer always knows what's there and what's safe to change.

We were building from scratch in a field none of us knew, so we tried a lot of flows and structures before one held up.

Status: delivered to 2ft as a hi-fi prototype. Their own team builds it from here, so we weren't part of the rollout.

How we measured it: all 5 people we interviewed (the president, treasurer and three volunteers) ran the same set tasks on the old spreadsheets and on the prototype, saying aloud what they saw and what they'd do next. We counted the clicks each task took and asked a single ease rating after every task. Across clicks per task and those ease ratings, we project updates about 70% easier than on the spreadsheets.

What we'd track after launch: how long it takes a volunteer to answer "what do we have right now?", how many records new volunteers update in their first week, and how many duplicate entries remain.
