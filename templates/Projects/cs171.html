Unicode is why a letter typed on one phone arrives as the same letter on another. When it fails, you get an empty box (□) instead. It works so well that most people never think about it, so our job was to make them notice.

{% endcall %} {% call cs.section("Design Challenge", lead="Too technical and people leave. Too simple and it’s a Wikipedia page.") %}

Our audience was everyone in CS 1710, not designers or engineers. Every chart had to make sense to someone who had never heard the word “encoding”, without boring the people who had.

{% endcall %} {% call cs.section("Exploration", lead="We started with fonts, and the data said no") %}

Our first proposal was about typefaces in the digital age, aimed at graphic and UI/UX designers. My storyboard asked whether typography is drifting into a monoculture, since our early research found about ten fonts behind over 60% of web text.

The datasets didn’t hold up. Web font numbers from 2015 to 2024 were rich, but there was almost nothing solid on how people feel about fonts. The idea was more “visual” than “visualization”.

{% endcall %} {{ cs.mockups([ (img('ux', 'cs171/map_data.png'), "Exploration map of candidate datasets"), (img('ux', 'cs171/map_audience.png'), "Exploration map of possible audiences"), (img('ux', 'cs171/map_q.png'), "Exploration map of guiding questions"), ]) }} {% call cs.media_text(img('ux', 'cs171/outline.png'), "Storyboard sketch showing the narrative arc and planned interactive slides for the data story", heading="Convergence", lead="So we pivoted to Unicode") %}

After a check-in with the course staff, we kept the part of the idea that had real data behind it: how text encoding grew. ASCII → Unicode → emoji → writing systems gave us a start, a twist (emoji are Unicode too!) and an ending anyone could relate to.

{% endcall %} {% call cs.section("Research", lead="Seven datasets, one sentence each") %}

For my half I gathered seven datasets, from World Bank literacy rates to Emojipedia’s record of the first emoji set and Unicode’s own publication history. Cleaning them meant filling or dropping pre-1990 gaps, fixing non-standard country codes and lining up release dates.

Our rule: if we couldn’t explain a chart in one sentence, it needed a better label, a better visual, or both.

{% endcall %} {% call cs.section("My Contribution", lead="The emoji half: data, a bubble chart and a game") %}

Rain took the story up to 2010: ASCII, the Unicode timeline and the Sankey of Unicode’s growth. I took everything after, which is mostly emoji.

The decisions I made, and why:
— Each emoji is a bubble; click one to see how its design differs across platforms and years. It comes after the simpler charts, so readers already know the toggles and filters it uses.
— Filters look like radio buttons, so it’s obvious from the start that the emoji can be filtered.
— A slider sets how many emoji float on screen at once, so the chart never turns into soup.
— “What emoji are you (right now)?” gets people clicking before the heavier charts.

{% endcall %} {% call cs.section("Prototyping", lead="Games on paper before charts in code") %}

We sketched quiz and sorting games before building any charts, to see which interactions taught something and which only moved.

Our words were.") %}

We ran two think-aloud tests on the second prototype. Apart from one missing y-axis, the charts held up. Our words didn’t: a “version” of what? What does “sort by code value” mean? Why is there a wall of text here?

{% endcall %} {{ cs.compare([ {"title": "What we observed", "points": [ "“Version” and “code value” meant nothing without context.", "Long text between charts got skimmed, and the definitions with it.", "One tester assumed emoji were part of ASCII, because the transition never said otherwise.", "The floating emoji looked random at first glance.", ]}, {"title": "What we changed", "points": [ "Acronyms spelled out, labels rewritten in plain words.", "The big text block became a clickable timeline.", "A transition slide that says it plainly: emoji are Unicode too.", "Radio-style filters and a slider for how many emoji are on screen.", ]}, ]) }} {% call cs.section("Outcome", lead="Live, and the message landed") %}

The story went live as our CS 1710 final in fall 2025. It runs from ASCII constraints to Unicode's growth, emoji and writing systems.

{% endcall %} {{ cs.stats([ ("2 of 2", "think-aloud testers got the main message"), ("7", "datasets I sourced and cleaned after we pivoted to Unicode"), ]) }} {% call cs.section() %}

One tester said everything was “extremely clear” and worked out every control on her own. The other got the gist but stalled on terms like “version” and “code value”, and on the long text between charts.

What I'd measure next: whether a first-time reader can say what Unicode is after one pass, and where people stop scrolling.

Next iteration, I’d strengthen onboarding even further: a short guided “tour,” clearer transitions that explain why each section matters, and more progressive disclosure (show basics first, advanced controls second) to keep first-time users confident.

I also want to focus more on accessibility: ensuring all interactive elements are keyboard navigable, animations respect user preferences, and color choices meet contrast standards.
