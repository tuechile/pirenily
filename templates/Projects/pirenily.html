How I Built This Website

Emily is my English name. I picked it in sixth grade, my brother agreed, and then nobody ever used it. I tried again in college and everyone said I don’t sound like an Emily. It stayed mine anyway. Iron Pig was my fan fiction author name: I loved Iron Man, and my middle school classmates called me a pig because I eat too fast. Emily + Iron Pig = Pirenily.

The search bar on the landing page flashes through the rest of my names: ones people call me, ones I call myself, and ones nobody calls me at all. Chi Le, Chi Chi Cha Cha, Le Le. Emily Tiles, which is Chi Le with the Ch as a T (also, I like tile textures). Chi Bell, which is what people call me a lot in Vietnam. Kai, which is how some people read my name; I’m not Greek. And The Country, yes, like Chile. Type anything into it and it finds the closest page, typos and all. If nothing matches, it opens a random one.

I’ve always been a designer, but I didn’t know code before the US. In Vietnam, “IT” class meant Microsoft Office certification tests. Finding out code could be used for art made AFVS 55P my favorite class, and the best first class I could have asked for.

{% call cs.row("equal", caption="From AFVS 55P: Kill the Pig, and the doll from Heart Room in the dark.") %} {{ cs.card(img('afvs', 'p1-final.jpg'), "Kill the Pig: the finished sculpture with a face projected onto it") }} {{ cs.video_card(img('afvs', 'chud darkmode.mp4')) }} {% endcall %}

By the end of that semester I had a pile of interactive projects, and a CS50 final project to make. I didn’t want to build something I’d never use again, and the idea of an archive has always fascinated me. So: I want to be a niche artist, let’s build a niche, weird website, and keep everything in it.

I’m a maximalist, but I don’t want to be maximalist about one thing. I want to be everything at once. So the frame stays minimal, and the work gets to be everything.

The first version went up on December 8, 2024, built in one sitting: 32 commits between 3:37 in the afternoon and 10:57 at night. AI was bad at design back then, which was perfect. It couldn’t make a single design decision for me, so I planned everything myself and made ChatGPT build exactly what I described. Most of what defines the site was already there that night: the names, the loader between pages, the fonts. This is it, still up as it was:

Before it, there was my high school one, a photography portfolio already called Pirenily:

{{ cs.site("https://pirenily.com/pirenily-highschool/", "My high school website", ar=1.6) }} {{ cs.img_caption("My high school site, live. Code on GitHub.") }}

It hasn’t changed that much, because it still says what I want it to say. Even the loader is the same one.

Ideas show up all day: in class, looking at a poster, anywhere. I don’t write them down. Writing cleans out the stuttering and the fast talking, and then I can’t remember what I meant. So I talk: voice-to-text, and voice notes to my brother. When he replies, I have to defend the idea, and I can feel what I felt when I said it. Building happens later, when my brain is off. The thinking is already done, so I can hyperfixate and just make it.

There are two views, and you switch between them with the ☀ in the nav. Try it now. The dark one is mine. It grew out of artists I love, people from the School for Poetic Computation, Alicia Nguyen, Wyatt Roy’s 2024 site, Baku89, and Chia Amisola most of all: minimal, but still a little overstimulating. Her old website is the most beautiful thing I’ve seen on the internet.

At some point he told me it was starting to look vibe coded. He was right: I’d let Claude make too many design decisions for me. So I stepped back. Pinterest, my old art, websites I used to love. I asked what each template was doing, what I could cut, and what would make it look like me again. Then I wrote my decisions down in Pirenily UI, so the next time I doubt one, I can see why I made it and change it on purpose.
